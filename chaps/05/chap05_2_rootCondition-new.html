Root Condition and Zero-Stability

6.2. Root Condition and Zero-Stability#

It is possible to derive any number of multistep methods of various order, but the most important factor related to each method is its stability property.

Definition 6.1 (Stability)

Stability of a numerical method is its ability to control the growth of perturbations, such as round-off and truncation errors, during the computation. A stable method ensures that these errors decay or remain bounded so that they do not grow uncontrollably and significantly corrupt the numerical solution.

The general form of a \(k\)-step linear multistep method can be written as

(6.1)#\[ \sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_i y_{j+i}~=~ h\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_i f_{j+i} \qquad \text{with}~ \alpha_k =1, \]

and the exact solution \(y(x_{j+i})\) and its first derivative \(f(x_{j+i})=y'(x_{j+i})\) satisfies a similar equation given by

(6.2)#\[ \sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_i y(x_{j+i})~=~ h\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_i f(x_{j+i}) + C_p h^p y^{(p)}(x_j), \]

where \(C_p h^p y^{(p)}(\xi)\) is the leading truncation error of the method.

Subtracting equation (6.1) from equation (6.2) gives

(6.3)#\[ \sum_{i=0}^{k}\alpha_i \biggl( y(x_{j+i}) - y_{j+i} \biggr)~=~ h\sum_{i=0}^{k}\beta_i \biggl( f(x_{j+i}) - f_{j+i} \biggr) + C_p h^p y^{(p)}(\xi), \]

Let \(h \rightarrow 0\), we get a homogeneous linear difference equation for error terms \(e_j, e_{j+1}, \ldots, e_{j+k}\)

(6.4)#\[ \alpha_0 e_j + \alpha_1 e_{j+1} + \alpha_2 e_{j+2} + \cdots + \alpha_k e_{j+k} = 0, \]

where \(e_{j+i}=y(x_{j+i}) - y_{j+i}\) is the error at \(x_{j+i}\). The associated characteristic equation of (6.4) is

(6.5)#\[ \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 z + \alpha_2 z^2 + \dots + \alpha_k z^k = 0. \]

  • If equation (6.5) has \(k\) non-repeated roots \(z_1 \neq z_2 \neq \ldots \neq z_k\), then the general solution to equation (6.4) is

    (6.7)#\[ e_j = c_1 z_1^j + c_2 z_2^j + \cdots + c_k z_k^j. \]

  • If equation (6.5) has \(k\) repeated roots \(z_1 = z_2 = \ldots = z_k = z\), then the general solution to equation (6.4) is

    (6.7)#\[ e_j = ( c_1 + c_2 j + c_3 j^2 + \cdots + c_k j^{k-1} ) z^j. \]

See Section 2.4 for more details on difference and characteristic equations.

Question

What kind of conditions shall we impose to keep the error \(e_{j}=y(x_{j}) - y_{j}\) bounded for \(j = 1, 2, 3, \ldots, n, \ldots\)?

Definition 6.2 (First and Second characteristic polynomials)

For a general linear multistep method

\[ \sum_{i=0}^k \alpha_i y_{j+i} = h \sum_{i=0}^k f_{j+i}, \]

we call

(6.8)#\[ \rho(z) = \sum_{i=0}^{k} \alpha_i z^i \]

the first characteristic polynomial, and

(6.9)#\[ \sigma(z) = \sum_{i=0}^{k} \beta_i z^i \]

the second characteristic polynomial.

Definition 6.3 (Root Condition)

Let \(\,z_1,z_2,\dots,z_k\,\) denote the roots of the first characteristic polynomial \(\,\rho(z)\,\) associated with a linear multistep method. If \(\,|z_i|\leq1\,\), for each \(\,i=1,2,...,k\,\), and all roots with absolute value \(1\) are simple roots (i.e. not repeated), then the method is said to satisfy the root condition.

Fig. 6.1 Unit circle in the complex plane \(\C\).

Definition 6.4 (Zero-Stability)

A linear multistep is zero-stable if its first characteristic polynomial \(\,\rho(z)\,\) satisfies the root condition given in Definition 6.3, i.e. the roots of \(\,\rho(z)\,\) lie inside or on the unit circle, and the roots on the unit circle are distinct.

  • A zero-stable method is strongly stable if all the roots of \(\rho(z)\) are inside the unit circle except for only one root, called principal root, with the magnitude of one (i.e. \(|z| = 1\)).

  • A zero-stable method is weakly stable if it but has more than one root on the unit circle.

Note

In electrical engineering and control theory, the right hand side of a first-order ODE

\[\diff{y}{x}=f(x, y)\]

is called the input of the system. In this context, we can interpret zero-stability as the stability of the system with zero input.

We use the root condition to assess whether the discretised system (difference equation) can remain stable under zero input condition.

Example 6.2

Find the roots associated with the following characteristic polynomials:

  1. \(\rho(z)= z^3 + z \)

    Solution (click to show)

    Let \(z^3 + z ~=~ 0\)
    \(\therefore\quad z(z^2 + 1) ~=~ 0\)
    \(\therefore\quad z_1 ~=~ 0, ~~ z_2 ~=~ i, ~~z_3 ~= -i\)

    We can also use Matlab to find the roots of the polynomial

    p=[1 0 1 0];     %vector defining the coefficients of the polynomial
roots(p)         %solve the polynomial equation

    Output

    ans =

    0.0000 + 0.0000i
    0.0000 + 1.0000i
    0.0000 - 1.0000i

    \(z_1\), \(z_2\), and \(z_3\) are shown on the unit circle. Because there are more than one root on the unit circle, the method is weakly stable.

  2. \(\rho(z)=z^4 + z\)

    Solution (click to show)

    Let \(z^4 + z ~=~ 0\)

    \[\begin{split} \begin{aligned} z^4 + z & = z(z^3 + 1) \\ & = z (z+1) (z^2-z+1) \\ \end{aligned} \end{split}\]

    \(\therefore\quad z_1 ~=~ 0\), \(z_2 = -1\),  \(z_3 = \dfrac{1}{2} + i\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\),  \(z_4 = \dfrac{1}{2} - i\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\).

    We can also use Matlab to find the roots of the polynomial

    syms z
solve(z^4+z)

    Output

    ans =
                -1
                0
1/2 - (3^(1/2)*1i)/2
(3^(1/2)*1i)/2 + 1/2

    Because there are more than one root on the unit circle, the method is weakly stable.
