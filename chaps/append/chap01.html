Learning objectives:

  • Create algebraic expressions from given contexts

  • Use BODMAS

  • Evaluate an algebraic expression by substitution, including expressions involving powers

  • Simplify expressions by gathering terms and expanding brackets

  • Factorise simple algebraic expressions

  • Divide algebraic expressions

What Is Algebra?#

By now, you should already be familiar with arithmetic. In arithmetic, we only deal with numbers and mathematical operations on them like addition, subtraction, multiplication and division. The knowledge of arithmetic is very useful for solving simple problems but it will not be sufficient for more general and difficult problems. For example, if we know that a number and its half added together become 15, what is that number? You may well know the answer to this question, but without some basic knowledge of algebra it will probably take you some time to find it. So what is algebra? Basically, algebra is a branch of mathematics that has been developed to manipulate symbols. In algebra we use letters (English or Greek alphabets for example) to represent numbers or quantities of interest. In this chapter, first we will look at how to create simple algebraic expressions from given contexts and then look at some techniques that we can use to simplify them.

Symbols used in algebra essentially represent quantities (with or without units) that could be known already or unknown yet. They can be constants, or variables, whose values can change from case to case.

For example, we can use \(g=9.8~ \mathrm{m/s}^2\) to represent the gravitational acceleration on the Earth, we can use the letter \(n\) to represent the number of students in a class.

Creating Algebraic Expressions#

Why do we use letters to stand for quantities?

Let’s look at some examples

Example 1

Errol buys \(\boldsymbol{x}\) apples and \(\boldsymbol{y}\) oranges. Apples are 39p each and oranges are 56p each. How much does he spend? $\(\left .% \{ \begin{tabular}{ll} \text{Total spent} =& ~ \text{the number of apples that Errol buys times 39 pence} \\ & + \text{the number of oranges that Errol buys times 56 pence} \end{tabular} \right \} \label{eq:1.1}\)\( If we use the letters \)\boldsymbol{x}\( and \)\boldsymbol{y}\( to represent, respectively, the number of apples and the number of oranges that Errol buys, we can write the total spent as \)\(39x+56y \quad \text{(units in pence)} \label{eq:1.2}\)$

Suppose that Errol buys \(\boldsymbol{z}\) pineapples as well and they are £1.6 each, the total cost is now $\(39x+56y+160z \quad \text{(units in pence)} \label{eq:1.3}\)$

The £1.6 has to be expressed as 160p so that we are adding together pence throughout the whole expression. In general, we can only add things of the same type.

When internet calls at off-peak time are charged at 3p per minute then the cost of a call can be calculated from the formula $\(\text{Cost (in p)} = 3 \times \text{time on call (in minutes)}\)$ This ensures that if we double the time on call we will double the cost. For instance the cost of a 20 minute call is 60p. If we double the time to 40 minutes the call doubles to 120p.

In mathematics we could reduce this to $\(\label{eq:1.4} C=3\times t\)$ Remember that the symbols represent the cost and the time and the corresponding units are pence and minutes.

These are examples of the simplest form of algebraic notation. The abbreviation using symbols saves time and enables us to concentrate on the important issues associated with the formula. In fact in algebra when two values are placed side by side we assume they are to be multiplied so we would normally write the formula in [eq:1.4] above as $\(C=3t\)$

The quantities \(\boldsymbol{x}\), \(\boldsymbol{y}\) and \(\boldsymbol{z}\) in [eq:1.2] and [eq:1.3] for cost are variables; they stand for any one of a range of possible values. A variable may be given a specific value when we want to work out a particular sum.

Similarly, in [eq:1.4], the values for \(t\) and \(C\) will change from call to call and so they are also called variables. We tend to think we can choose the time to be any value within reason. The cost then depends on the value we have chosen. Because of this we call \(t\) the independent variable and \(C\) the dependent variable.

[eq:1.2] defines a function for calculating the cost in terms of the variables \(\boldsymbol{x}\) and \(\boldsymbol{y}\), which works whatever values are given to \(\boldsymbol{x}\) and \(\boldsymbol{y}\).

We use variables and functions to describe general properties. This has huge advantages over specifying properties in a multitude of individual specific cases. From the general cases, we can establish patterns which can be used to solve other similar problems.

Because variables represent numbers, we manipulate expressions involving variables using the rules of arithmetic.

Substitution and BODMAS#

In the previous section, we have created a number of algebraic expressions. In Example [example:1A], the cost for Errol to buy \(\boldsymbol{x}\) apples and \(\boldsymbol{y}\) oranges is represented by the algebraic expression $\(39x+56y.\)\( If at one time he buys 5 apples and 4 oranges, how much will he pay? To work out this we simply replace \)\boldsymbol{x}\( with number 5 and \)\boldsymbol{y}\( with 4 and then the total cost will be \)\(39 \times 5+56 \times 4=195+216=419~ \text{(pence) or} ~4.19~ \text{(pounds)} .\)$

This process of replacing letters (symbols) in an algebraic expression with actual numbers and then working out the final numerical value is called evaluation by substitution.

Evaluate \(2x+4y+10\) when \(x=1.4\), \(y=2.2\).

Substituting \(x=1.4\) and \(y=2.2\) into \(2x+4y+10\), we have $\(\begin{aligned} & 2x+4y+10 \\ =~ & 2\times 1.4+4\times 2.2+10 \\ =~ & 2.8 + 8.8 + 10\\ =~ & 21.6 \end{aligned}\)$

In algebra we use letters or symbols to represent numbers so the operations on them must follow the same rules that numbers obey.  The BODMAS rule tells us the order in which the mathematical operations must performed.

If an expression contains two operations with the same priority, we evaluate by working from left to right.

  1. To evaluate an expression such as $\(11-3+14,\)$ we would follow the BODMAS rule giving 22 as the answer.

    Since addition and subtraction are of equal priority we scan the expression from left to right calculating as we go.

    Similarly to find the value of  \(a+b-c\)  we first add \(a\) to \(b\) and then subtract \(c\).

  2. When dealing with a numerical expression such as $\(3+5 \times 2\)$ we perform the multiplication before the addition (to get 13).

    So when evaluating the algebraic expression $\(a+bc,\)\( we would find the value of \)b \times c\( first and then add the value of \)a$.

  3. To override the normal rules of precedence we use brackets.
    Thus to calculate $\(6 \times (2+8)\)\( we perform the addition before the multiplication. This gives an answer of 60. Likewise to find the value of \)\(a(b+c)\)\( we first add \)b\( to \)c\( and then multiply the result by \)a$.

Since algebraic symbols only follow the rules of numbers if a rule does not work for numbers, it does not work for algebra either.

To add fractions we do not simply add the denominators and the numerators. This would give us the wrong answer. For instance,

\(\dfrac 1 2+\dfrac 1 3\) does not equal \(\dfrac{1+1}{2+3}=\dfrac 2 5\)

so in algebra \(\dfrac a c+\dfrac b d\) is not equal to \(\dfrac{a+b}{c+d}\)

Powers in Algebra#

In Arithmetic, when a number is multiplied to itself several times, we can use a short-hand form called power or index to represent it. For example, $\(2\times 2\times 2\times 2\)\( can be written as \)2^4\( (2 raised to the power of 4). Similarly, in algebra we write \)a^2\( for the product \)a\times a\(,   \)a^3\( for \)a\times a\times a$, and so on. The calculation of a power takes precedence over multiplication and division.

To find the value of \(5a^2\) we first calculate a2 and then multiply by 5 so, if \(a=3\), \(a^2=9\) and \(5a^2=45\).

If we had wanted to perform the multiplication first, we would have written \((5a)^2\). Since \(a=3\), \(5a=15\) and

\((5a)^2=15\times 15=225\).

Take care here. Failing to make the calculations in the right order is a very common error.

Roots#

When a number multiplies itself, we can use power to represent the operation, for example $\(3\times 3 = 3^2 = 9\)\( Here we know \)9\( is a square number. And we call \)3\( a square root of \)9\(, and write this as \)\(\sqrt[2]{9}=\sqrt{9}=3\)\( At the same time \)\((-3)\times (-3) = (-3)^2=9,\)\( so \)-3\( is also a square root of \)9$.

A positive number has two opposite square roots. For example \(5\) and \(-5\) are the square roots of \(25\). A real number has only one cube root.

In general, if we have $\(\underbrace{a \times a \times a \cdots a \times a }_{n} = a^n = b\)\( then the \)n^\text{th}\( root of \)b\( is \)\(\left\{ \begin{aligned} ~ \sqrt[n]{b}, \quad & n~ \text{is odd}, \quad & b \in \mathbb{R} \\ ~ \pm\sqrt[n]{b}, \quad & n~ \text{is even}, \quad & b \geq 0 \\ \end{aligned} \right.\)\( We can write the \)n^\text{th}\( root of \)b\( as \)\(\sqrt[n]{b}=b^{\frac{1}{n}},\)$ so

  • \(\sqrt{9}=9^{\frac{1}{2}}\)

  • \(\sqrt[3]{8}=8^{\frac{1}{3}}\)

  • \(\sqrt[10]{x}=x^{\frac{1}{10}}\)

Gathering Like Terms and Expanding Brackets#

Algebraic expressions can sometimes be shortened by combining terms. One instance of this can arise when the same term appears twice in a sum. Thus we can simplify

\(a+a+9\)  to  \(2a+9\)

We can still simplify the expression if one or both of these terms are multiplied by a constant or if the terms are subtracted.

  1. \(2a=a+a\)  and  \(3a=a+a+a\) \({\Rightarrow}\)  \(2a+3a=5a\)

  2. \(6b=b+b+b+b+b+b\)  and  \(4b=b+b+b+b\)  \({\Rightarrow}\) \(6b-4b=2b\)

We call this process “gathering like terms”. Algebraic expressions may contain two or more sets of like terms and constants also.

In the expression   \(8x+5y+2+3x-y-3\)

\(8x\) and  \(3x\) are like terms  and 5y and y are like terms. Gathering like terms together we get $\(8x+3x+5y-y+2-3\)\( which simplifies to  \)11x+4y-1$.

Be careful not to combine unlike terms in this way. We cannot simplify \(11x+4y-1\) any further.

When creating algebraic expressions, frequently we use brackets to group some quantities together. To simplify certain algebraic expressions, such as, \(1.2(2x+4y)\), sometimes we need to remove all the brackets in them first. To do this, you need to use the number or letter in front of the brackets to multiply every term inside them. This process is called expanding the brackets. For example, after expanding the brackets, the above expression will become \(2.4x+4.8y\). Notice that the \(1.2\) multiplies every term in the brackets not just the first one. This remains true when there are more than two terms in the brackets and when the terms are negative.

  1. \(2(p+q+r)=2p+2q+2r\) (Not \(2p+q+r\))

  2. \(5(2x^2-y^2)=10x^2-5y^2\)

When there is a negative term outside the brackets, it is important to remember that the product of two negative terms is positive. This includes the case where there is only a subtraction sign preceding the brackets, as in Example [example:1K] (d) below. In practice, this implies there is \(-1\) before the brackets. Getting the signs wrong, when expanding brackets, is another very common error.

Once we have expanded two or more sets of brackets we may be able to gather like terms.

  2. \(\begin{aligned}[t] 13x-(x-7) & = 13x-x+7 \\ & = 12x+7 \end{aligned}\)

Simple Factorisation#

The reverse process of ‘expanding the brackets’ is called factorising. An example will suffice.

If we expand \(10(2x+3y)\) we get   \(20x+30y\).
If, however, we start with the expression \(20x+30y\) and rewrite it as the product of two factors

i.e.  as \(10(2x+3y)\),

we say the expression has been factorised.

We can only factorise terms when they have a common factor, for instance, we could not factorise \(15x+14y\) because none of the factors of the first term, 3, 5 and \(x\) are factors of the second term.

Spotting the common factor is the essential first step in the factorisation process. Once the process is completed we can always check our answer by expanding the brackets to see if we obtain the original expression.

  1. All the terms in the expression  \(3x+6y-18z\)  have a factor 3.
    To find the second factor we divide each term by 3 giving us \(x+2y-6z\).
    Hence we believe that $\(3x+6y-18z=3(x+2y-6z).\)\( If we now expand the brackets we get \)3x+6y-18z$, so our factorisation is indeed correct.

  2. The term \(2x\) is common to all the terms in the expression  \(8xy+6x^2\).
    Dividing each of the terms by \(2x\) we get \(4y+3x\). Hence we think that $\(8xy+6x^2=2x(4y+3x).\)$

Further Expansions#

We normally write the product of two or more sets of terms in brackets without the multiplication sign. Thus the expression \((x+2)\times (x+3)\) is written \((x+2)(x+3)\). When expanding such an expression involving two pairs of brackets we must multiply every term in the first bracket by every term in the second.

General Method#

  • Two brackets:
    The general rule for expanding two brackets is that every term in the first bracket should multiply every term in the second bracket. Of course, we can also treat one bracket as a single symbol, then deal with the expression as one bracket multiplying one symbol. For example $$\begin{aligned} (a+b)(c+d)&=a(c+d)+b(c+d) \ &=ac+ad+bc+bd

    \end{aligned}$\( here we treat \)(c+d)\( as a single symbol first, and let it multiply every term in \)(a+b)$.

  • Multiple brackets:
    To expand multiple brackets, we can iteratively expand two brackets. For example $$\begin{aligned} (a+b)(c+d)(e+f+g) ~=~&(ac+ad+bc+bd)(e+f+g) \ ~=~&ace+ade+bce+bde \ ~&~+acf+adf+bcf+bdf \ ~&~+ acg + adg + bcg +bdg

    \end{aligned}$\( here we firstly expand the first two brackets \)(a+b)(c+d)\(, then expand the remaining two brackets \)(ac+ad+bc+bd)(e+f+g)$.

Factorising \(Ax^2+Bx+C\)#

Supposing a general quadratics \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) can be factorised as $\(Ax^2+Bx+C=(ax+b)(cx+d),\)\( we can expand the two brackets to find the relations between the coefficients \)\(\begin{aligned} (ax+b)(cx+d)&=acx^2+adx+bcx+bd \\ &=acx^2+(ad+bc)x+bd \\ &=Ax^2 + B x + C. \end{aligned}\)$ We get the following clues:

  1. \(A=ac\)

  2. \(C=bd\)

  3. \(B=ad+bc\)

  4. \((ad)\cdot(bc)=A\cdot C = (ac)\cdot(bd)\)

Using clues (iii) and (iv), we can work out the factorisation through the following steps:

  1. Multiply \(A\) and \(C\), denote the result as \(D=A\cdot C\)

  2. Factorise \(D\) as \(D=B_1 \cdot B_2\), and make sure \(B_1 + B_2 = B\)

  3. Split \(Bx\) as \(Bx=B_1 x + B_2 x\)

  4. Factorise \((Ax^2+B_1 x)+(B_2 x+C)\)

Factorise \(2x^2+5x+3\)

Solution:

  1. \(2\times3= 6\)

  2. \(6=1\times 6\), \(6=2\times 3\), \(2+3=5\)

  3. \(5x=2x+3x\)

  4. \(\begin{aligned}[t] 2x^2+5x+3&=2x^2+2x+3x+3\\ &=(2x^2+2x)+(3x+3)\\ &=2x(x+1)+3(x+1)\\ &=(2x+3)(x+1) \end{aligned}\)

Factorise \(2x^2+3x-2\)

Solution:

  1. \(2\times(-2)= -4\)

  2. \(-4=1\times (-4)\), \(-4=-1\times 4\), \(-4=-2\times 2\), \(-1+4=3\)

  3. \(3x=-x+4x\)

  4. \(\begin{aligned}[t] 2x^2+3x-2&=2x^2-x+4x-2\\ &=(2x^2-x)+(4x-2)\\ &=x(2x-1)+2(2x-1)\\ &=(x+2)(2x-1) \end{aligned}\)

Laws of Indices#

The laws of indices listed below can be used for simplifying many algebraic expressions involving powers. For examples, the first law says that to multiply two numbers with the same base we can simply add the indices and the second law states that to divide two numbers with same base we can subtract the indices.

On the other hand, the sixth law gives the definition for negative powers. According to this law, for example, \(2^{-3}\) means \(\dfrac{1}{2^3}\) , that is \(\dfrac{1}{8}\).

.& \((b^n)^m = b^{nm}\) & \((x^3)^4 = x^{3\times 4}= x^{12}\)
.& \(b^1 =b\) & i.e. power 1 can be omitted.
.& \(b^0 =1\) & this gives the definition for zero power.
.& \(b^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{b^n}\) & \(x^{-8} =\dfrac{1}{x^8}\)

Polynomial and Polynomial Division#

A polynomial is an algebraic expression involving a sum of powers in one or more variables multiplied by coefficients. A polynomial in one variable (i.e., a univariate polynomial) with constant coefficients is given by $\(\label{eq:polynomial} a_n x^n + a_{n-1} x^{n-1} + + a_{n-2} x^{n-2} \cdots + a_2 x^2 + a_1 x + a_0.\)$

  • In a polynomial, the indices should be whole numbers (non-negative integers) only.

  • The highest power in a univariate polynomial is called its degree, or sometimes its order.

  • \(a_n\), \(a_{n-1}\), \(a_{n-2}\), …, \(a_2\), \(a_1\) and \(a_0\) are constants and they are called the coefficients of the polynomial.
